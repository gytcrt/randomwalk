"Why do I start a startup?"

This one question guides me through uncertain and discouraging moments. I figure it might be helpful to structure my thoughts and note my answers down. Beside serving as a reference for myself (or my reader), I'm also curious to see whether my answers change over time. If so, how?

## Seeking unpredictability

When I was a teenager and in my early twenties, I liked setting up three year or even five year goals for myself—I worked towards coming to the US for college, moving to NYC as a young professional, and getting promoted in a prestigious firm in Hudson Yards. My life had been largely on track of my plan and I felt proud of it.

Gradually, I realized if I stay in Corporate America, my life would become so predictable that I could already imagine how my days and years would play out from now to my sixties. This exact predictability was what drove me away from my {{< newtabref href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chongqing" title="hometown" >}} in China—I had always dreamed of an exciting future in an unfamiliar place, and never wanted an {{< newtabref href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_rice_bowl" title="iron rice bowl" >}}. Even the rigorous planning in my teens and twenties was a means to an end, in pursuit of establishing myself in this foreign land (NYC, USA).

I like the unpredictability of not calculating how much my 401K account will be like in ten years, and I love dreaming big. I know I will regret retiring comfortably in my sixties without having attempted to build something outside of Corporate America. Therefore, I'm building a startup.

## Feeding curiosity

If you have met me in person or are my friend, you know I'm inquisitive. Sometimes my friends and family get annoyed by my endless questions. A startup is the perfect playground to feed my curiosity.

I'm curious about how this world operates, why things happen in certain ways, and how people interact with the world and each other. For example, when a consulting firm charges its client $1000 for a project, I'd like to know how the price is structured, what value the client gets from the project, and how I should quantify it. If I work for an established company or even a startup with a handful of customers, I would not have a full picture of the market or economics of the business to answer those questions.

Now that I'm running a startup, I need to have an answer (or at least a hypothesis) for everything around my business, including but not limited to:

* Are we in an AI bubble? What does this environment mean for our startup?
* How do companies with customer service departments operate differently across industry, size, and geography?
* What is our value as a company economically and philosophically?
* What color theme or words should we use on our website?
* Should we hire an engineer in NYC, or South America, or Asia?
* And so forth

I wouldn't have to answer those questions if I were an employee, as they are often above an employee's pay grade. But I want to confidently know the answers, so I embark upon the journey of a startup.

## A sense of ownership

Two years ago, I bought a schoolhouse pendant for my study. When the pendant was delivered to my building, I considered hiring a handyman to mount it up. After the seller sent me an installation manual and reassured me it should be relatively easy to do it myself, my partner and I took on this task—neither of us had any experience with mounting light fixtures before this. It took us three hours on a thundering weekend afternoon to install the pendant, and I almost lost my temper when I struggled to screw the new fixture to the old distorted base on the ceiling.

{{< figurelightbox src="pendant.jpg" caption="The schoolhouse pendant in my study" alt="A schoolhouse pendant light fixture" align="center" width="100%" >}}

I was so frustrated at one point during the installation, but now I feel satisfied whenever I look up and see the classic schoolhouse pendant. The startup journey is similar—I need to own, plan, and execute many tasks. When I overcome obstacles and accomplish them, the sense of growth and achievement is unmatchable.

## Impact on my surroundings

Living in a society where work is highly specialized and compartmentalized, I used to feel disconnected from the impact of my work and a lack of control over my work environment. For example, when I prototyped a sleek AI solution to a Fortune 500 company and delivered the prototype along with an impeccable deck, I often didn't know how my work tangibly impacts the client. Or when my trusted manager wanted to help speed up my immigration process, he had to jump through many internal policy and organizational hoops. After all that, he still couldn't make things different for me.

By starting a company from scratch, I can see how my product solves the client's problem and know they are happy with it; I can own and create a supportive environment for my team.

"Impact" can be a cliche and oversold word. But being able to see my impact on my immediate environment gives me a sense of autonomy and motivation. It's my way to fight against modern alienation.

## Last but not the least

I spent a couple of weeks thinking about and drafting this article in my spare time. I do have many more obvious or philosophical answers to the question, but I find the four reasons above most relevant whenever I have doubts.

I'm happy to share more if you ask me in person.
